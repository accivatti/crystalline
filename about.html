The Beginning

It started in a Detroit kitchen, not a restaurant.

Charlie Accivatti grew up in Detroit, in a house where meals were not a pause between other things but the thing itself. He watched what happened when food was right: who relaxed, who stayed longer, how a conversation changed once the plates arrived. That observation became a question he spent his career trying to answer professionally.

He did not start out wanting to be a chef. He started out wanting to understand how restaurants work and why most of them don't. The kitchen was the beginning of that education, not the end of it. He moved across roles on purpose: line cook, floor manager, food and beverage director, catering company founder, restaurant consultant. Each position taught him what the others could not see from the inside.

He wanted to understand how food costs constrain a menu, how a poorly run floor can wreck a perfectly cooked dish, how the decision made in September about staffing shows up in service the following June. That cross-discipline knowledge is what made him useful in a restaurant opening and what makes him useful now.

By the time he was leading openings, he had spent enough time on every side of a hospitality operation to know where problems originate. Almost always in decisions made before the first guest arrives. Thirty years in, that is still the first thing he looks for.

Career Scope

Opening Restaurants Across North America and Beyond

A restaurant opening has exactly one opening night. Before it arrives, everything that can go wrong has to be found and fixed: the staffing gaps, the menu items that will not hold under service pressure, the supplier relationships that sound solid until they are not. Charlie has run that process multiple times, in markets that each demanded something different.

Coral Gables put him in rooms with guests who had eaten in great restaurants on three continents and would notice anything that fell short. Dallas and Irving showed him what it takes to serve quality at real scale: hundreds of guests eating simultaneously, none of them feeling like they are at a catered event. Mexico City made clear how much food culture shapes a room: that the sourcing, the timing, and the pace of service are cultural decisions as much as operational ones. And Fez, where hospitality predates the modern restaurant industry by several centuries, changed what he understood welcoming someone to a table to mean.

He brought all of it back with him.

Companies & Roles

What He Built Along the Way

Founder · Sold 2008

Smart Catering

Charlie founded Smart Catering to close a gap he had seen throughout his career: operations producing good food on systems too inefficient to sustain quality at scale. Smart Catering was built from the ground up around operational infrastructure as much as culinary craft. It grew into a respected operation and was sold in 2008. The conviction behind it, that great hospitality requires great operations, runs through everything he has built since.

Managing Director

appétit

As Managing Director of appétit, a SaaS food delivery platform, Charlie brought his operational expertise to the intersection of hospitality and technology, two industries that often speak past each other. The role put him inside questions of systems design, user experience, and the mechanics of service at a scale no kitchen can reach on its own. It reinforced a belief he has always held: the warmth a guest experiences at the table is the result of a thousand decisions made before they arrived.

Co-Owner · Currently Operating

Nur Restaurant, Fez

Nur Restaurant in Fez, Morocco is among the most meaningful chapters of Charlie's career, and the one still being written. Operating in one of the oldest medinas in the world, Nur takes Moroccan hospitality seriously as a living practice. Not as an aesthetic. As a way of running a restaurant. Opening a restaurant in the Fez medina is not the same as opening one in Dallas. You are not introducing something new. You are earning a place in something much older.

Science & the Table

What a career at the stove and a Harvard invitation have in common.

As a guest presenter at Harvard's Science of Cooking program and at the World Science Festival, Charlie brought the practitioner's perspective to conversations usually dominated by researchers. He was not there to validate the theory. He was there because the stove had already answered many of the questions the researchers were asking.

These were not honorary appearances. They were substantive contributions to public conversations about how cooking works at a molecular and chemical level, and what that understanding means for the way professional chefs approach their craft.

For Charlie, science and cooking have always been the same discipline. Both are about understanding cause and effect well enough to predict outcomes and create something repeatable. That rigor applies equally to a restaurant opening and to a pasta workshop in Buffalo, where participants learn not just how to make pasta but why it works: why resting the dough matters, why the finished texture reflects decisions made long before the pot hits the water. Understanding the why is what turns a recipe into a skill.

Western New York

Coming Home to Buffalo

Charlie grew up in Detroit and built his career in markets he was not from: kitchens and dining rooms across North America, and eventually Morocco. Buffalo is where he chose to land, and he has spent years here making that a deliberate investment rather than an accident of geography.

What he brought with him was something most chefs working in Western New York simply did not have: firsthand experience in hospitality operations that held a very high standard, in cities where that standard was not negotiable. The guests he serves now do not need to travel to experience a dinner with the precision of a great restaurant in New York or the warmth of Nur in Fez. They can have it at their home, at their event, at their table, in Buffalo.

Western New York has always had the appetite for this. What it needed was someone who had worked at that level elsewhere and decided to stay.

Today

Crystalline Management

Crystalline Management is Charlie Accivatti's private hospitality company, based in Buffalo, New York. It serves clients across Western New York and travels for events that call for it.

The work ranges from intimate private dinners for eight to full-room weddings, from wood-fired pop-up pizza events to pasta workshops where the goal is to teach something that lasts longer than the evening. Every engagement is designed from the ground up around the specific occasion. No set packages. No catering-hall shortcuts. Menus are built around the event, the season, and the people being fed.

What Crystalline Management offers is professional hospitality, directed at whatever you are gathering people for. That is not a marketing claim. It is what this work looks like when it is pointed at your table.

Philosophy

On Mentorship and the People Behind the Food

No one builds a career in hospitality alone. The cooks who taught him knife skills before he was trusted with anything else. The general managers who showed him that a dining room is a living thing, responsive to mood and energy. The chefs in Morocco and Mexico who extended their trust and let him watch how they worked.

Mentorship runs in both directions in his operation. The team at Crystalline Management is trained not just on technique but on the philosophy behind it: why presentation matters, why timing matters, why the guest's experience from arrival to departure is a continuous conversation that the hospitality team is either improving or degrading. There is no neutral.

That conviction, that hospitality is something you do actively and not passively, is the thread running through everything Charlie has built. It is what brings him back to the kitchen for a private dinner in Orchard Park with the same energy he brought to a restaurant opening in Mexico City.

The table is always worth setting well.

— Charlie Accivatti
